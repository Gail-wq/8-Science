    Science skills

    The skills we use in every topic: planning a fair test, processing data, writing conclusions and answering test questions at Achieved, Merit and Excellence. These are the habits that get you ready for NCEA science later on.

    Read the command word, then pitch your answer

    LevelCommand wordsWhat your teacher is looking forSentence starter
    AName, state, define, describe, label, calculateCorrect science words and facts. What happens.“A solution is… It is made when…”
    MExplain, give reasonsHow or why it happens, using science ideas (particles, forces, charge) and the context in the question.“This happens because… which means…”
    EDiscuss, compare, justify, evaluateTwo or more ideas linked together, using data, with consequences or a judgement.“Therefore… In contrast… This shows that…”

    The “because chain”

    Most Merit and Excellence marks are lost because the explanation stops too early. Push every point through four links:

    What→How→Why→So what?

    Example: “Snowshoes have a large area (what), so the weight is spread out (how). Pressure = force ÷ area, so a bigger area gives a smaller pressure (why). The snow can support the lower pressure, so the person doesn’t sink (so what).”

    Planning a fair test

    • Aim: what are you trying to find out?
    • Independent variable: the ONE thing you change on purpose.
    • Dependent variable: the thing you measure, with units.
    • Controlled variables: everything else you keep the same.
    • Hypothesis: “I think that as ___ increases, ___ will ___ because ___.”
    • Repeats: at least three, so you can take an average and spot odd results.

    Processing data

    • Average = add the repeats, then divide by how many there are. (4.8 + 5.2 + 5.0) ÷ 3 = 5.0 s.
    • Round sensibly: usually to the same number of decimal places as your measurements.
    • If one repeat is very different from the others (an outlier), check it and consider leaving it out of the average.
    • Always include units in table headings, not in every box.

    Graphs

    • Title: “The effect of [independent] on [dependent]”.
    • Independent variable on the x-axis, dependent on the y-axis, both with units.
    • Column graph for categories; line or scatter graph when both variables are numbers.
    • Use an even scale that fills at least half the grid.

    Writing a conclusion

    • State the overall pattern: “As ___ increased, ___ decreased.”
    • Quote the best and worst results with numbers and units.
    • Calculate the difference between them.
    • Say whether your hypothesis was supported.
    • Explain the science behind the pattern.

    Weak: “8 mL was best.” Strong: “8 mL gave the longest average flight time of 5.0 s, compared with 3.5 s for 12 mL, a difference of 1.5 s.”

    Evaluating an investigation

    • Reliable: were the repeats close together?
    • Fair: did anything else change apart from the independent variable?
    • Errors: reaction time, reading scales, uneven amounts.
    • Improvements: say exactly what you would change and why it would help.

    Common mistakes from our 2026 tests

    • Saying a solution is “cloudy”. Solutions are clear; suspensions are cloudy.
    • Saying the solute “disappears” when it dissolves. Its particles spread out between the water particles.
    • Mixing up pollination (pollen moving) and fertilisation (gametes joining).
    • Saying moths “changed colour to survive”. Individuals don’t change; the population changes over generations.
    • Saying protons move when objects are charged. Only electrons move.
    • Calling every change with bubbles “chemical”. Boiling water bubbles but is physical.
    • Thinking a moving object must have an unbalanced force. Constant speed means balanced forces.
    • Forgetting units: N for force, Pa for pressure, A, V and Ω for electricity.
    • Not showing working in calculations. Write the formula, the numbers, then the answer with units.
    • Confusing mass (kg) and weight (N).

    Before a topic test

      How we learn in Year 8 Science

      Study toolkit

      The routines behind this site, and how to use them at home. Each one is based on strong evidence about how memory and understanding build.

      Retrieval practice

      Pulling knowledge out of memory strengthens it far more than re-reading. Start every study session with the Do Now on the home page or a flashcard round, before you open your notes.

      Spaced practice

      Revisit each topic after 1 day, 3 days, 1 week and 3 weeks. Earlier topics (mixtures, traits) need a short weekly review even while we are learning about forces.

      I do, we do, you do

      Worksheets move from a worked example, to a partly completed one, to a task on your own. If the task is hard, step back to the worked example rather than guessing.

      Dual coding

      Pair words with diagrams. Draw a flower, a circuit or a particle diagram from memory with labels, then write the explanation next to each label.

      Blurting

      Set a 5-minute timer and write everything you know about one success criterion. Check against the notes in a different colour. The gaps are your study list.

      Self-assessment

      Use the Self-check tab on each topic to traffic-light the success criteria. Your ratings save on this device so you can track progress across the year.

        For teachers: how the resources are designed

        • Learning intentions and success criteria for each topic are written at A, M and E level so students can see the progression.
        • Example answers show the same question at all three levels, with a note on what lifts each answer to the next level. Students attempt first, then compare.
        • Worksheets give immediate feedback (check buttons) and can be printed as clean handouts.
        • Flashcards and the Do Now draw on one shared glossary, supporting interleaved retrieval across topics.
        • Contexts are local where possible: the Waikato River, Huntly Power Station, dairy farms, kōwhai, tūī and kererū.
